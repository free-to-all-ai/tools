On the phone with them right now?

  1. Hang up. That's OK to do. A real bank, government office, or family member will understand.
  2. Don't send money, read out gift card numbers, or let anyone into your computer.
  3. Call back at a number you already know is real: the back of your bank card, a paper bill, or your family member's own phone number. Don't use a number the caller gave you.
  4. If you feel unsafe, or someone is on the way to pick up cash or gold, call 911 or your local police.

4. Who to call in Michigan

If money left your account, start at the top. Reporting helps even if the money is gone: it helps catch the people doing this.

Your bank or credit union

Use the number on the back of your card

Call first if any money left your account. Ask them to stop or reverse the payment and protect your accounts. For a wire, ask for a "recall" and a "hold harmless letter." The FBI says doing this fast may cut or erase your loss.

Federal Trade Commission (FTC)

ReportFraud.ftc.gov or 1-877-382-4357

Report a scam or a scam call, even if you didn't lose money. The phone line is 1-877-FTC-HELP. The website works in English, Spanish (ReporteFraude.ftc.gov), and a dozen other languages. The FTC shares reports with over 2,000 law enforcers. It can't solve your single case, but it uses reports to bring cases.

FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)

ic3.gov (online form)

The FBI's place to report scams by phone, text, email, or online, and anything with crypto. The FBI says to file "even if you are unsure of whether your complaint qualifies." Put in every payment detail. The IC3 will never call you first or ask you for money.

Age 60 or older? Get free help filing. The U.S. Justice Department's National Elder Fraud Hotline: 1-833-372-8311 (1-833-FRAUD-11), Monday to Friday, 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. Eastern. English, Spanish, and other languages. A family member can call for you, and the case manager can help fill out the IC3 and FTC forms.

What to write down for your report

  • The date and time of each call, text, email, or visit.
  • Every phone number, email address, website, app, and name they used.
  • Each payment: the date, the amount, and how you paid. For a bank or wire, the account it went to. For crypto, the transaction ID, the wallet address, and where the crypto ATM is.
  • Keep receipts, gift cards, wire slips, and crypto ATM receipts. Take screenshots or photos of texts and messages.

Watch out for a second scam

After a scam, people often hear from someone who promises to get the money back for a fee. The FBI says the IC3 "will never directly contact you for information or money" and doesn't work with law firms or crypto services to get money back. The FBI also warns about crypto "recovery services, especially those charging an up-front fee." Scammers have even pretended to be the National Elder Fraud Hotline. If anyone contacts you about getting money back, hang up and call the real number on this page.

Worried a parent or older relative is being scammed?

The FBI lists these signs that someone may be caught up in a crypto ATM scam. Many fit other scams too:

  • Large cash withdrawals, especially for the first time, often asking for big bills.
  • QR code papers they can't explain.
  • Seeming confused or nervous when asked why they need the cash, or talking about a new "financial advisor" who wants cash.
  • Talking on a cell phone and seeming confused while taking out cash or using a crypto ATM.

Talk without blame. The FBI's message to older adults is: "Do not be afraid to report a suspected crime." Go through this checklist together, and call the bank together if money went out. The National Elder Fraud Hotline (1-833-372-8311) can help you report for a loved one who can't do it alone.

If the person taking money is someone they know, call Michigan Adult Protective Services at 855-444-3911.

How this works. Each box you check is linked to warning signs written by the FTC, the FBI, or the Michigan Attorney General. The page shows those signs in plain words with a link to where each one comes from. It also checks your boxes against the FTC's four signs of a scam: they pretend to be from a group you know, they say there's a problem or a prize, they pressure you to act now, and they tell you to pay in a certain way. Red cards are steps to take now. Amber cards are warning signs. Green cards are ways to check. The page never decides whether something is a scam. Only you, your bank, or the police can find that out.
