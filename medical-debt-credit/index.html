Medical Debt on Your Credit Report Check

Medical bills in collections can show up on your credit report. But Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion say they leave some of them off: ones you paid, ones that started under $500, and ones less than a year old. Federal law adds a 7-year limit and a right to dispute. List your medical debts to see which ones shouldn't be there, then draft the dispute letters.

This is information, not legal or financial advice. The bureaus' medical debt rules are their own voluntary policy, not a law, and they can change. Every rule here was checked against its source on . Nothing you type here leaves this page or gets saved.

3. Your letters

One dispute letter for each credit bureau that shows a debt with something to dispute, and one letter asking each collector to prove the debt. They fill in from your answers above. You can edit them right in the box.

The bureaus use it to find your file.

Before you send them

  1. Fill in anything in [brackets], then sign each letter. The bureaus also ask for your Social Security number to find your file. Write it in by hand.
  2. Add copies, never originals: the page of your credit report with the item circled, proof you paid, your insurance Explanation of Benefits, or anything else that backs you up. Equifax's form also asks for a copy of an ID and a bill that shows your address.
  3. The CFPB suggests certified mail with a return receipt, so you can show when it arrived. Keep a copy of everything.
  4. You can also dispute online or by phone with each bureau. A mailed letter leaves a paper record.

How this works. For each debt, the page checks your answers against two things. First, the three credit bureaus' own announced policy on medical collection debt: paid in full, an initial reported balance under $500, or less than one year past due means it isn't included. That is voluntary policy, not law. Second, federal law: the Fair Credit Reporting Act's 7-year limit and dispute rights, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act and Regulation F on collectors, and the IRS rules that nonprofit hospitals must follow before reporting a patient to a credit bureau. Green cards are what the rule says directly. Amber cards depend on your facts. Red cards mean act now. The page can't see your credit report, so the result is only as good as the dates and amounts you enter.

Dates: the one-year wait is counted from the date the bill first went past due. The 7-year limit is counted the way 15 U.S.C. 1681c(c)(1) says, starting 180 days after that date. The 30 days to dispute with a collector is counted from the day you got its first notice.
